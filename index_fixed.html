How a Liquid Rocket Engine Works

The Big Picture

A liquid rocket engine turns stored chemical energy into thrust by burning a fuel and an oxidizer together in a combustion chamber, then accelerating the hot gas through a nozzle. Because it carries its own oxidizer, it works in the vacuum of space as well as in the atmosphere — it never needs to breathe outside air.

Fuel & Oxidizer

Two propellants are stored in separate tanks: a fuel (such as kerosene, hydrogen, or methane) and an oxidizer (typically liquid oxygen). They're pumped at high pressure into the chamber in a ratio — the oxidizer-to-fuel ratio (O:F) — tuned for the most efficient, stable combustion.

Turbopumps & the Combustion Chamber

Turbopumps spin at tens of thousands of RPM to force propellant into the chamber far faster than gravity ever could. Inside the chamber, the propellants atomize, mix, and burn continuously at temperatures exceeding 3000°C, generating enormous chamber pressure that has nowhere to go but down, through the throat.

Nozzle Expansion

The converging-diverging (bell) nozzle accelerates exhaust gas to supersonic speed. The expansion ratio (exit area ÷ throat area) determines how far the gas expands before leaving the nozzle. A nozzle sized for vacuum overexpands at sea level, losing efficiency — which is why rockets are shaped differently for atmospheric and space flight.

The Thrust Equation

Thrust comes from momentum leaving the engine, plus a small correction for the pressure difference between the exhaust and the surrounding air:

F = ṁ · Ve + (Pe − Pa) · Ae

where ṁ is the propellant mass flow rate, Ve is exhaust velocity, and the second term accounts for exit vs. ambient pressure acting on the nozzle exit area Ae.

Specific Impulse (Isp)

Specific impulse measures propellant efficiency — how much thrust you get per unit of propellant burned per second. Higher Isp means a rocket can achieve more velocity change from the same amount of propellant.

Isp = Ve / g₀

Mass Flow & the Rocket Equation

As propellant burns, the vehicle gets lighter, so the same thrust produces more acceleration over time. The total velocity a rocket can gain is governed by the Tsiolkovsky rocket equation:

Δv = Isp · g₀ · ln(m₀ / mᵤ)

where m₀ is the initial (wet) mass and mᵤ is the final mass after all propellant is spent.
